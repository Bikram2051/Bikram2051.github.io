The [first episode]({% post_url 2026-11-22-why-most-australians-earn-less-than-the-average-wage %}) of Median Australia found the employee in the middle earning about $1,425 a week. The second asks the obvious next question. Is that keeping up with prices?

The short answer: over ten years the middle got a little ahead. Since 2021 it has slipped back. And how far back depends on whose prices you count.

## Start with $80,000

Say you earned $80,000 in September 2021, and your pay rose in line with the Wage Price Index, the ABS measure of pay for the same job. Five years later you would be on about $95,100. To buy what $80,000 bought back then, you would need:

<dl class="keyline">
  <div><dt>$99,700</dt><dd>to keep up with the Consumer Price Index</dd></div>
  <div><dt>$105,100</dt><dd>to keep up with the ABS living cost index for employee households</dd></div>
  <div><dt>$113,100</dt><dd>in our mortgage scenario, built on our own assumptions</dd></div>
</dl>

Same pay rise, very different gaps: about $4,600, $10,000 and $18,000 a year, before tax. The rest of the episode works out where each number comes from.

## Ten years: a little ahead

The median employee earned $1,000 a week in August 2015 and $1,425 in August 2025. Consumer prices rose 33% from September 2015 to September 2025, so keeping up would have taken about $1,330 a week. The median got to $1,425. That is about $95 a week ahead after prices, roughly 7% over the decade.

But the median is only one way to measure pay. Over the same ten years, after prices, the four common measures give four answers:

| Measure | Ten years after prices |
|:--|--:|
| Wage Price Index (the same job) | down 2.6% |
| Average full-time earnings | up 1.9% |
| Median weekly pay | up about 7% |
| Median hourly pay, to 2024 | up 7.7% |

They differ because they answer different questions. The Wage Price Index holds each job fixed, so promotions, job changes and people moving into better-paid work never show up in it. The median catches all of that. One asks whether the same job pays more. The other asks what the middle worker earns.

## Since 2021: slipping back

The ten-year gain was all in place by 2021. In 2025 dollars, the median was worth about $1,439 a week in 2021, using the CPI. In 2025 it was $1,425: $14 a week less. Not much, honestly.

Change the price index, though, and the answer changes. Against essentials only, the middle earner is $51 a week behind. Against the ABS living cost index for employee households, $76 behind, about 5%.

## Which prices went up

From September 2021 to September 2026 the CPI rose 24.6%. Not everything rose at that pace. Gas rose almost 50%, insurance 47%, electricity 36% even after government bill rebates, fuel 32%, council rates 28%, rents 27% and food about 25.5%. Energy, insurance, fuel and housing: the bills you cannot really skip.

A note on that rent figure. It is the ABS measure of the rent people actually pay across all current leases, new and existing. Advertised rents for new leases are a different measure.

## An essentials index, checked against the ABS first

So we built an essentials index of our own from ABS prices for groceries, rent, electricity, gas, water, council rates, fuel and insurance, weighted the way the ABS weights them. Together they are about 27% of household spending.

Before believing it, we tested the method. We rebuilt the whole CPI the same way, from the same raw data, and over five years the rebuild landed within a twentieth of a percentage point of the official figure. The ABS also publishes its own non-discretionary inflation series: 5.2% in the year to September 2026, against 4.3% for the headline CPI. Our index, built separately, also came out at 5.2% for that year.

Over five years, our essentials index rose 29.4% against the CPI's 24.6%. One honest caveat: widen essentials to include health, schooling, childcare and phone bills, and the rise drops to 25.6%. The squeeze is in the core bills.

That check is the part I would defend hardest. A homemade index proves nothing on its own. Reproducing the official number first is what earns its other answers a hearing.

## The cost the CPI leaves out

The CPI tracks the price of buying things, including new homes, but by design it does not count the interest on a home loan. The ABS publishes a second set of measures, the Selected Living Cost Indexes, that do. One of them is for employee households, where wages are the main source of income.

Over the five years to September 2026, employee households' living costs rose 31.4%. Same-job pay, measured by the Wage Price Index, rose 18.8%. Against the CPI that leaves same-job pay 4.6% behind. Against employee living costs it is 9.6% behind, about double. The big driver is mortgage interest: the ABS measure of mortgage interest charges for employee households rose about 178% in those five years.

## Three households

Not every household has a mortgage, so the episode runs three scenarios. They use our assumptions, not official figures, and they are all on screen.

| Household | Same-job pay over five years |
|:--|--:|
| Owns outright, no rent or mortgage | 3.2% behind |
| Renter, with rent a quarter of spending | 4.1% behind |
| Mortgage holder, with interest 12% of spending in 2021 | 16% behind |

If the mortgage share was anywhere from 8% to 16% of spending, the answer runs from about 12% to 19.5% behind.

A smaller gap does not mean an easier time. The Reserve Bank's [Financial Stability Review](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/fsr/2026/oct/resilience-of-australian-households-and-businesses.html) notes that most mortgage holders have savings and equity buffers, while lower-income households, many of them renters, are more likely to feel stress because their expenses take a larger share of their income.

## The latest year

In the twelve months to September 2026 the Wage Price Index rose 3.5%, the CPI 4.3% and employee living costs 5.1%. On every one of those measures, same-job pay is still behind.

## What it does not show

- The dollar figures are before tax, and they assume every dollar of pay goes on living costs. If you save some of your pay, your gap in dollars is smaller.
- The three households are illustrations built on our assumptions, not anyone's real budget.
- None of the official numbers is wrong. They measure different things. The gap is in which question you ask.

The biggest cost behind the gap is housing, so the next episode looks at what it takes to rent or buy a home on a median wage.
