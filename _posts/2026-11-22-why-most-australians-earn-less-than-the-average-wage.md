This month I started a YouTube channel called Median Australia. The idea is in the name: report the middle, not the headline. Each episode starts from the files the Australian Bureau of Statistics publishes, rebuilds the number that makes the news, and asks what it means for the person in the middle.

The first episode asks the plainest question I could think of. What does the typical Australian actually earn?

{% include youtube.html id="PnMeck8WoIA" title="What does the typical Australian earn? Average vs median wage | EP01" %}

## The number in the headlines

The figure usually called the average wage is real. The ABS puts average weekly ordinary time earnings for full-time adults at $2,083.70 in May 2026. Times 52, that is about $108,000 a year.

But read the label. Full-time workers only. Adults only. Ordinary hours, so no overtime. And an average, not the middle. None of that is a mistake: the ABS is measuring what full-time adult work pays. The trouble starts when the number is called the average wage, as if it described everyone.

## Three corrections, one month

To compare like with like, the episode uses one month, May 2025, when the headline was $2,010 a week.

<dl class="keyline">
  <div><dt>$2,010</dt><dd>The headline: average ordinary earnings of full-time adults</dd></div>
  <div><dt>$1,611</dt><dd>The average once every employee counts, part-time, casual and overtime included</dd></div>
  <div><dt>$1,436</dt><dd>The median: the employee exactly in the middle</dd></div>
</dl>

Counting everyone who works brings the average down to $1,611 a week. Then stop averaging. Line every employee up from the lowest pay to the highest and take the one in the middle: $1,436 a week, about $74,700 a year. That is 29% below the headline, and about 70% of the gap comes from the first step alone.

Before trusting it, one check. The $1,436 comes from the ABS survey of employers. The ABS also asks households, in August, and that survey's median was $1,425. Two different methods, $11 apart.

## Where the headline sits on the ladder

Pay has a floor but no ceiling. A few very large pay packets pull the average up, and the middle does not move. The household survey shows the whole ladder for August 2025:

| Rung | Weekly pay |
|:--|--:|
| One in ten earn less than | $450 |
| One in four earn less than | $900 |
| The middle (median) | $1,425 |
| The top quarter starts at | $2,127 |
| The top tenth starts at | $3,000 |

So where does the headline land? In the employer survey for May 2025 the median was $1,436 and the top quarter started at $2,122. On a straight line between the two, the headline of $2,010 sits at about the 71st percentile. That is where "about seven in ten employees earn less than the average wage" comes from. It is my estimate, not a published figure, and the true share is probably a little higher, because earnings thin out above the middle.

## Your middle is probably not the national middle

The national median mixes very different people. The biggest single factor is hours. In August 2025 the median full-time employee earned $1,741 a week. Among part-timers it was $650 for men and $750 for women. Among full-timers it flips: $1,841 for men and $1,631 for women, a $210 gap every week.

Age is the next biggest. Teenagers with a job had a median of $300 a week, and eight in ten of them work part-time. Every age group from 25 to 59 sat above the national median, which is pulled down by more than two million employees under 25. So for someone aged 35 to 44 the fair benchmark is about $1,700 a week, or $1,950 if they work full-time.

Place and job move it too. The ACT had the highest median of any state or territory at $1,600 a week, and Tasmania and South Australia the lowest at $1,300. By industry the spread is much wider: $2,761 in mining, $700 in accommodation and food services. A lot of these gaps are hours rather than hourly pay. Per hour, the median was $42.90.

## Who sets the pay

There are three main ways pay is set in Australia. In May 2025, 38.5% of employees had an individual arrangement with their employer, 34.6% were on a collective (enterprise) agreement, and 22.7% were paid exactly the award rate.

Award-only workers averaged $922 a week, partly because seven in ten of them work part-time. Per hour, non-managerial staff on the award averaged $36.20, against $50.00 on individual arrangements and $53.70 on enterprise agreements. The jobs are different, though, so this is a map of who is paid which way, not proof that one method pays better.

## Tax returns tell a lower story

The tax office counts pay differently again. On 2023-24 tax returns the median salary or wage income was $65,046, and median taxable income across 16.58 million returns was $58,739.

That is below the $74,700 worked out above because a tax return covers a whole year, including people who only worked part of it, and because it comes from an earlier year. The ABS figures are one week of pay, multiplied by 52. Neither is wrong. They answer different questions.

## Ten years of the middle

The median employee earned $1,000 a week in August 2015 and $1,425 in August 2025, 42.5% more. It did not move smoothly. It sat at $1,000 from 2014 to 2016, jumped 7.6% in the year to August 2024, then rose just 1.9% in the latest year.

All of that is before prices. Whether the middle actually got ahead is the question for [the second episode]({% post_url 2026-11-22-is-your-pay-keeping-up-with-prices %}).

## Which number to use when

- To check whether your pay is normal, compare it with the median for people like you: full-time or part-time, your kind of job, your state.
- When you read about the average wage, ask three things. Full-time only? Average or median? Which month?
- When you turn a weekly figure into a yearly one, remember it assumes you worked every week.

## What it does not show

A median does not follow one person. The middle employee of 2025 is a different person from the middle employee of 2015. That is partly why the median rose even in 2020: the ABS notes that job losses fell mostly on casuals, younger workers and lower-paid industries, so the middle of whoever was left moved up. The age figures are the same kind of snapshot. They describe different people at different ages in one month, not one person's career.
